# JTBD — Moesta Canon (Practitioner Reference)

Distilled from Bob Moesta's framework (primary source: Lenny Rachitsky interview, 2023-08-24, plus Demand-Side Sales 101, The Re-Wired Group). Load on demand when running JTBD work.

---

## 1. Core Reframe: Context, Not Pain

Moesta's single biggest correction to mainstream JTBD use:

> "I think one of the biggest misconceptions around Jobs to Be Done is this notion that it's pain and gain as opposed to context and outcome. When you hear somebody's story and it seems irrational... nine times out of 10 it's because you don't have the rest of the story."

> "Only people have jobs, products don't have jobs, people have jobs, organizations don't have jobs, people in organizations have jobs because that's the irrational part."

Practitioner implication: a "struggling moment" is not a "pain point." Struggling moments include time pressure, social stakes, incomplete information, circumstantial triggers. Pain is the negative valence only. The job is **causal**, not correlational.

The Snickers vs. Milky Way illustration: same shelf, near-identical ingredients, completely different jobs. Snickers competes with Red Bull, coffee, sandwiches (missed-meal, urgent energy). Milky Way competes with a glass of wine or a quiet run (regrouping after emotion). Supply-side analysis cannot see this distinction. Only demand-side can.

## 2. Who To Interview — The Hard Rule

**Only people who have already switched.** No aspirational respondents.

> "I never trust anybody telling me things they're going to do because they can't assure it, and it usually never happens."

> "Bitchin' ain't switchin'."

Three valid pools:

1. **Recent buyers** — hired your product. Ask why today was the day.
2. **Recent churners** — fired your product. They're still making progress, just elsewhere. They reveal where the struggling moment exceeded what you could handle.
3. **Competitor switchers** (zero-to-one) — switched to the closest analog. "What are people going to fire when they hire me?"

The Basecamp lesson: users loudly demanded Gantt charts and threatened to leave. None left. Building to satisfy stated complaints would have destroyed Basecamp's actual job (simplicity). JTBD protects against this.

## 3. The Six-Phase Buying Timeline

The arc a switch interview traces. Distinct from the Four Forces diagram.

| Phase | What Happens |
|------|--------------|
| 1. **First Thought** | Initial registration that "something is not good enough." Usually misremembered; must be excavated. |
| 2. **Passive Looking** | Problem-aware, solution-unaware. Notices what they used to ignore. |
| 3. **Active Looking** | Problem- and solution-aware. Investing time. Framing what "good" looks like. |
| 4. **Deciding** | Trade-offs crystallize. Hire/fire criteria lock in. |
| 5. **First Use** | Onboarding reality vs. expectations — the "little hire." |
| 6. **Ongoing Use** | New habit forms. New struggling moments emerge. |

Meeting prospects in the wrong phase is expensive. (Moesta's Autobooks example: splitting the demo into three versions for story/alternatives/choices phases cut sales cycle in half and quadrupled conversion.)

## 4. Interview Technique — No Discussion Guide

> "It drives people crazy because everybody wants to ask the same set of questions, but the problem... is when you ask the same set of questions, you actually don't follow the ones that actually have the most meaningful information in it."

Stance: **"criminal and intelligence interrogation that feels like therapy"** (Moesta's phrase). The Four Forces are the analytic frame; the interview is an improvised excavation aimed at causation.

### Bracketing Technique

When the interviewee runs out of language, offer **two alternatives you know are both wrong**: "Was it more about X or more about Y?" They'll say neither and then explain what it actually was. The correction is the data.

### Play-It-Back-Wrong

Deliberately mischaracterize the timeline back to them. "So you did this and this and this." They correct: "No, that wasn't it." The elaboration on why it isn't right surfaces the real causal mechanism.

### Layers of Language

| Layer | Content | Action |
|------|---------|--------|
| 1. Pablum | "How was your day?" "Oh, good." | Keep digging. |
| 2. Fantasy/Nightmare | "It was SO great" / "SO terrible." | Move past. |
| 3. What actually happened | Specific, contextual, causal. | This is the data. |

### Anti-Patterns

- Don't ask "why" directly — ask "tell me more," "give me an example," "walk me through that"
- Don't fill silence
- Treat anomalies as the richest data, not outliers ("Anomalies Wanted" — Christensen's office sign)

## 5. The Four Forces (Equation)

For the switch to happen: **F1 + F2 > F3 + F4**

- **F1 — Push of the Situation:** What is wrong with the current state. Not the new product. If there's no push, the person literally cannot see your product.
- **F2 — Pull of the New Solution:** Vision of a better outcome. A feature, a peer story, a promise.
- **F3 — Anxiety of the New:** Fear created by the new solution. "Will it work? Will my team adopt it? What if I'm wrong?"
- **F4 — Habit of the Present:** Inertia of the existing solution. Even people who hate it have habits built around it.

> "If there's no push, they can't even see your product because we're creatures of habit."

**Counter-intuitive intervention:** adding features often *increases* anxiety ("can it really do all those things?"). Reducing F3/F4 (friction) is frequently more effective than increasing F2.

The condo example: cancellations were driven by F3/F4 (anxiety about possessions, habits around larger living). Raising the price to bundle moving + 2 years of storage increased sales 30%. No product change.

See `forces-of-progress.md` for elicitation moves per force.

## 6. The Job Statement Format

**When [situation/context], I want to [motivation], so I can [desired outcome].**

Three energies that must show up in any complete job:

- **Functional energy:** time, effort, speed, knowledge
- **Emotional energy:** how the person feels (frustrated, overlooked, anxious, proud)
- **Social energy:** how others perceive the person, or how they want to be perceived

Synthesis only — not a question template. Don't ask interviewees to fill in the blanks. Write the statement *from* the data after interviews, then test by playing it back to other switchers.

**Examples:**
- Snickers: "When I've missed a meal and have work to do, I want to get calories fast and cleanly, so I can get back to my tasks without losing momentum."
- Milky Way: "When I've just had an intense emotional experience, I want to take a quiet moment to regroup, so I can return to equilibrium."
- SNHU (returning adult students): "When I have responsibilities that prevent me from being physically present, I want credentials I can earn on my own schedule, so I can advance without disrupting my existing life."

## 7. How Many Interviews — Causal Saturation

> "From a causal mechanism perspective and from a set theory perspective... it starts to repeat around seven or eight. And I usually do 10, no more than 12. And I would rather do two rounds of 12 interviews than do 24 interviews."

The goal is not statistical significance — it's **causal saturation**. When the sets of forces start repeating across interviews, you have the patterns. Two rounds of 12 beats one round of 24 because round 1 generates hypotheses, round 2 tests them and targets coverage gaps.

**Cluster, don't segment.** Segments group by attributes (demographics, stated preferences). Clusters group by **causal pathways** — combinations of push + pull + anxiety + habit that recur. Clusters are actionable; segments are descriptive.

Most products carry 3-5 distinct jobs, often in conflict. Product decision: serve all, or pick one and design for it.

## 8. Hire / Fire Criteria

People don't buy products — they **hire** them to do a job, **firing** whatever was doing the job before (including doing nothing).

- **Hire criteria:** what the new product must do to be brought into their life
- **Fire criteria:** what the previous solution was doing (often imperfectly) that the new product must preserve or explicitly discontinue

Zero-to-one teams should especially ask: **"What are people going to fire when they hire me?"**

## 9. When JTBD Is NOT the Right Tool

Moesta is explicit. Three scenarios:

1. **No real choice exists.** Employer-picked health insurance. Default-assigned utilities. You won't surface causal data because no causal decision happened.
2. **Deep habitual behavior.** Pack of gum. 20-year-Tide users. The hire decision is not available to memory. (Exception: ask them to tell the story of *switching from* Tide to Gain — that's accessible.)
3. **Client wants a predetermined conclusion confirmed.** JTBD is hypothesis-building, not hypothesis-testing. Don't run it to validate what the team already believes.

> "If I try to help you build the case to make the jobs what you think they are, it doesn't work."

When JTBD isn't right, Moesta's alternatives: ethnography, friction mapping, prototyping alternative framings.

## 10. Moesta-isms (Vocabulary the Plugin Should Use)

- **"Struggling moment"** not "pain point"
- **"Context and outcome"** not "pain and gain"
- **"Demand side"** not "supply side"
- **"Hire / fire criteria"** not "requirements"
- **"Cluster, don't segment"**
- **"Choose what to suck at"** — pick the job; don't try to serve conflicting ones
- **"A kick-ass half is better than a half-ass whole"** (attributed to Jason Fried; Moesta endorses)
- **"JTBD is hypothesis-building research"** — not hypothesis-testing

## 11. Moesta vs. Christensen vs. Ulwick

| | Moesta | Christensen | Ulwick (ODI) |
|---|--------|-------------|--------------|
| Frame | Method (tactical) | Theory (strategic lens) | Prescriptive system |
| Data | Qualitative switch interviews | Conceptual | Quantitative survey (180-3000 respondents) |
| Output | Force diagram + job story | Disruption thesis | Prioritized outcome list by segment |
| Best for | "Why are they switching?" | "Where is this market going?" | "What should we build?" |

They are complementary, not competing. Run Moesta-style interviews to understand the emotional/contextual landscape, then ODI to quantify and prioritize across the market. See `odi-ulwick.md` for the complement.

The Twitter case study: they reportedly used Christensen's theory without Moesta's interview method — hypothesizing jobs in a conference room, which Moesta says guarantees you will be wrong.
