# Switch Interview Guide (Practitioner Reference)

A step-by-step guide to running a Moesta-style switch interview. Load on demand when the plugin is coaching a user through interview prep, execution, or coding.

---

## 1. Before You Pick Up the Phone

### Who to Interview — Hard Rule

**Only people who have actually made a switch.** No aspirational respondents. No "thinking about it" respondents.

> "Bitchin' ain't switchin'."

Three valid pools:

1. **Recent buyers** of your product — hired you. Ask why today was the day.
2. **Recent churners** — fired you. They're now making progress elsewhere. Highest-signal interviews you can run.
3. **Competitor switchers** (zero-to-one) — switched to the closest analog product. "What are people going to fire when they hire me?"

### How Many

10-12 interviews. Patterns start repeating at 7-8. Two rounds of 12 beats one round of 24 — round 1 generates hypotheses, round 2 tests them.

### What "Recent" Means

Ideal: within the last 30-60 days. Memory degrades fast. The transactional moment (signing up, swiping the card) overwrites the actual timeline. Beyond 90 days, you'll get rationalization, not causation.

### Two Listeners If You Can

One interviews (drives the conversation), one listens for forces (takes structured notes). Debrief immediately after.

---

## 2. The Anti-Discussion-Guide Rule

> "It drives people crazy because everybody wants to ask the same set of questions, but the problem... is when you ask the same set of questions, you actually don't follow the ones that actually have the most meaningful information in it."

**No discussion guide.** Have the Four Forces in mind as the analytic frame. The conversation is improvised excavation aimed at causation.

The interview goal: reconstruct the 6-phase timeline (First Thought → Passive Looking → Active Looking → Deciding → First Use → Ongoing Use). All probes are in service of that arc.

Stance Moesta names: **"criminal and intelligence interrogation that feels like therapy."** Investigator (causation-seeking) + therapist (warm, patient, comfortable with silence).

---

## 3. The Six-Phase Timeline — What to Excavate at Each Stage

| Phase | What You're Looking For | Sample Probes |
|------|------------------------|---------------|
| **1. First Thought** | Pre-trigger context. The moment they noticed something was off — often months before the purchase. | "Take me back to before you were even looking for this. What was happening in your life?" / "Was there a specific moment you remember thinking something needed to change?" |
| **2. Passive Looking** | What they started noticing. What sources entered their attention. Who they started listening to. | "When did you start paying attention to options? What kind of attention?" / "Who did you start talking to or reading?" |
| **3. Active Looking** | Real alternatives considered. Comparison criteria forming. | "Walk me through what you looked at." / "What else did you seriously consider? Why those?" / "What did you stop looking at? Why?" |
| **4. Deciding** | Hire/fire criteria. Trade-offs. Anxieties. Social check-ins. | "What finally made you pick this one?" / "What did you give up?" / "Who did you check with before deciding?" |
| **5. First Use** | Onboarding reality vs. expectation. The "little hire." | "What was the first day like?" / "When did you know it was going to work?" / "What surprised you?" |
| **6. Ongoing Use** | New habits forming. New struggling moments emerging. | "How does it fit into your routine now?" / "What do you wish it did differently?" |

## 4. Core Techniques

### Bracketing — The Edge of Language

When the interviewee runs out of words, offer **two alternatives you know are both wrong**: "Was it more about saving time, or more about looking competent to your boss?" They'll say "neither — it was actually..." The correction is the real data.

This works because forced-choice surfaces something stated choice will not. Don't ask "was it about time?" (leading), or "what was it about?" (open and unproductive when they're stuck). Bracket instead.

### Play It Back Wrong

Deliberately mischaracterize the timeline back to them. "So you did this, and then this, and then this." They will correct: "No, that's not how it happened." The elaboration on why your version is wrong reveals the actual causal mechanism.

### Layers of Language

Most interviewees move through layers. Don't accept early answers.

| Layer | Sounds Like | Move |
|------|-------------|------|
| 1. Pablum | "It was fine." "I just needed something better." | Don't accept. Probe specifics. |
| 2. Fantasy/Nightmare | "It was AMAZING." "It was a DISASTER." | Acknowledge, then ground: "Tell me a specific moment." |
| 3. What actually happened | Dated, specific, contextual | This is the data. |

### Don't Ask "Why" Directly

"Why" pushes into rationalization. Use:
- "Tell me more"
- "Give me an example"
- "Walk me through that"
- "What happened next"
- "Who else was involved"

### Treat Anomalies as Gold

> "When you hear somebody's story and it seems irrational... nine times out of 10 it's because you don't have the rest of the story."

Christensen kept a sign on his door: **"Anomalies Wanted."** The data point that doesn't fit the pattern is usually where the next pattern lives. Hunt for the rest of the story.

### Embrace Silence

Don't fill pauses. The most interesting content often surfaces after 5-10 seconds of silence — the interviewee is searching for the real answer.

## 5. Sample Probes by Force

See `forces-of-progress.md` for the full force-by-force breakdown. Quick reference:

**For PUSH:** "What was wrong before you were even looking?" / "Walk me back to the first moment you noticed something was off."

**For PULL:** "What did you see that made you think this might work?" / "Whose recommendation mattered?"

**For ANXIETY:** "What almost stopped you?" / "Who did you check with?" / "What did you do to reduce the risk?"

**For HABIT:** "What did you have to stop doing?" / "What workarounds did you give up?" / "What did you miss?"

## 6. Anti-Patterns to Catch Yourself On

| Anti-Pattern | What It Sounds Like | Why It Fails |
|--------------|---------------------|--------------|
| Leading the witness | "Would you say checkout was confusing?" | Confirms hypothesis instead of finding truth. Replace with: "Tell me what happened at checkout." |
| Future-hypothetical trap | "Would you use a feature that did X?" | Worthless data. People say yes to anything aspirational. |
| Expert trap | Over-explaining context, signaling "correct" answers | Interviewee performs for you. |
| Premature closure | Wrapping up before the doorknob moment | Best content often emerges when guard is down. Let the "informal" conversation happen. |
| Defending the product | Reflexively explaining why something works the way it does | You're not selling. You're learning. |
| Surface stop | Accepting "it was just easier" without probing | "Easier" is pablum. Excavate: easier than what, in what specific situation? |

## 7. Two Anonymized Interview Excerpts

### [SYNTHETIC EXAMPLE] — Switching from Jira to Linear

> **Interviewer:** Take me back to before you were even considering switching. What was going on?
>
> **R:** Honestly, it was just slow. Jira was always slow.
>
> **I:** When you say slow — was there a moment that really stuck with you?
>
> **R:** ...Yeah. I think it was a Tuesday morning. We had a release at 10am, and at 9:45 I was trying to move three tickets to "in progress" and the page just kept spinning. I had my manager standing behind me. I felt like an idiot.
>
> **I:** What were you thinking right then?
>
> **R:** That there had to be something better. Like — it can't be this hard.

Note the move from pablum ("just slow") to a specific dated moment with social stakes (manager standing behind). The push wasn't speed in the abstract — it was a specific public humiliation that crystallized the dissatisfaction.

### [SYNTHETIC EXAMPLE] — Switching from Things 3 to Sunsama

> **Interviewer:** What made today the day you finally pulled the trigger?
>
> **R:** I kept ending Sundays feeling like I'd lost the week.
>
> **I:** Was it more about not getting things done, or more about not knowing what to do?
>
> **R:** Neither really. It was — I had a clean inbox in Things every Sunday. I'd hit zero. And I still felt awful about the week. Like, the inbox-zero wasn't the right metric anymore.
>
> **I:** Tell me more about that.
>
> **R:** I think I outgrew the job. Things solved "what's on my plate" beautifully. But my problem was "what's realistic for this week given my actual calendar," and Things didn't even ask that question.

Note the bracketing move ("more about X or more about Y?") that surfaced the real frame: she hadn't lost faith in Things' execution — she'd outgrown the job Things solved. This is the Sunsama vs. Things insight in concentrated form.

## 8. After the Interview — Force Mapping

Immediately produce the force-mapping artifact (see `forces-of-progress.md` Section 4):

```
PUSH: ...
PULL: ...
ANXIETY: ...
HABIT: ...

HIRE CRITERIA: ...
FIRE CRITERIA: ...

ENERGIES:
  Functional: ...
  Emotional: ...
  Social: ...
```

After 8-12 interviews, **cluster, don't segment.** Look for recurring combinations of forces — the same push showing up with the same pull and the same anxiety. Each recurring combination is a job. Write each as: **When [context], I want to [motivation], so I can [outcome].**

Most products carry 3-5 distinct jobs. Often in conflict. The next strategic decision is which one to serve, and what to suck at.

---

## 9. When NOT to Run a Switch Interview

- The category has no real choice (employer-assigned health insurance)
- Deep habitual behavior with no recent switch event (20-year-Tide users)
- The brief is to confirm a predetermined conclusion (this isn't research, it's motivated reasoning)
